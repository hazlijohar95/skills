# Find what the customer is buying

Apply when a contract has more than one line, a fee for setup or implementation, services
next to software, or anything the customer receives before or after the main service.

List the promises. For each, ask two questions. Could the customer benefit from it on its
own, or with things it could readily get elsewhere? Is it separable from the other
promises in this contract, rather than an input to one combined thing? Promises that pass
both tests are separate performance obligations. Activities that transfer nothing to the
customer are not promises at all.

**Why.** Revenue timing attaches to performance obligations. Split one promise into two
and part of the price gets recognized early, when the lesser part is delivered. Merge two
and revenue waits for the slower one. The test is about the customer's view: what are they
buying, and what is just the entity getting ready to deliver it.

**Pattern.**
- Setup, provisioning, and data loading that only the entity can do, and that the customer
  cannot use without the subscription, are activities. They are not a service. The fee
  for them is an advance payment on the subscription (see options-can-be-promises for
  whether it runs past the term).
- Implementation that third parties also sell, that the customer could do itself, or that
  leaves the customer with something useful without the subscription is usually a
  separate promise.
- Significant integration, customization, or interdependence (the software is heavily
  modified by the services, or neither works without the other) makes them one promise.
- A subscription of identical periods (stand-ready access, the same seats each month) is
  one performance obligation made of a series of distinct periods. That matters later:
  variable amounts can attach to a single period, and modifications are usually
  prospective.
- The subscription starts when the customer can use it, not on the contract date. When
  setup gates access, the service period starts at access. The contract date is fine
  only when the gap is immaterial, and the memo says so with the figure.
- A right to take the software on-premise without significant penalty, and to run it
  there, makes the arrangement contain a license. The license is a separate promise,
  usually transferring at a point in time. Weigh the penalty both ways. Fees that keep
  running after the customer self-hosts, and updates that stop, point to a penalty (no
  license). No extra charge, and customers who already self-host, point away from one.
  A right to download "the current release at any time" may also be a promise of later
  versions. Test that as its own obligation.
- Minimum commitments drawn down by usage: decide whether the customer is buying access
  for the period (stand-ready, recognized ratably) or a quantity of units (recognized as
  used, with expected shortfall as breakage only when history supports it). Argue both
  in the memo. The pricing mechanics don't decide it. What the customer can do with the
  platform does.

**Deciding facts.** Whether anyone else sells the service. Whether the customer can use
the output without the subscription. What the services change about the software. The
on-premise clause, if any. `pricing.md` style standalone sales data.

**The tell.** Revenue booked when "implementation completes" for setup work that only
unlocks the subscription.

**Codification.** 606-10-25-14(b), 25-15 (series), 25-19 to 25-21 (distinct),
55-50 to 55-53 (upfront fees and setup activities), 55-54 (hosting and licenses).
