# Revenue follows control

Apply always. Every other principle is this one applied to a specific fact pattern.

Revenue is recognized when the customer gets control of what was promised, in the amount
the entity expects to be entitled to for it. Invoices, cash, and contract signatures are
evidence about that. They are never the event.

**Why.** A company can invoice early, collect late, sign in December for January service,
or bundle a year of work into one invoice. If revenue tracked any of those, it would
measure the sales team's paperwork, not the business's delivery. Control is the one thing
that moves only when the customer actually receives something.

**Pattern.**
- Ask what the customer can use, direct, or benefit from today that it could not
  yesterday. That is the transfer. Date the revenue there.
- Treat every schedule keyed to invoice dates as a hypothesis. Rebuild it from what was
  delivered and check whether the two agree.
- When billing and delivery match (monthly SaaS billed monthly for that month), the
  billing schedule is a fine proxy. Say why it matches. Don't assume it.
- The amount is what the entity expects to keep, not the invoice total. Credits, rebates,
  refunds, and bonuses move it.

**The tell.** A deferred revenue spreadsheet with one row per invoice spread over the
invoice's service dates. It is right for plain subscriptions and wrong whenever an invoice
carries more than one promise, a ramp, a fee for setup work, or a price that changes after
the fact.

**Codification.** 606-10-25-27 (over time), 606-10-32-5 (variable consideration). The
core principle of ASC 606 is not in the verified table. Cite it by name.
